Winning Starts
Before the Tender

The strategic foundations of a higher bid win rate

Amir Bercovitz  ·  byamir.ai

Foreword

You already knew you were going to lose.

Perhaps not consciously. But in hindsight, when you read the debrief — when the client explains why a competitor won — you recognise the signals that were already there. The client you barely knew. The win strategy that never really existed. The proposal the client described as "technically strong but generic".

I have guided tenders worth hundreds of millions of euros. Managed teams bidding on complex, international procurements — from mining in North and South America to facilities management for major global clients. The pattern is the same everywhere.

From practice

In a €12 million tender I was advising on, my client did not lose on price and did not lose on quality. They lost because the competitor had built a working relationship with the new Operations Director — the real decision-maker behind the formal procurement committee — three months earlier. My client knew the specifications perfectly. They did not know the person behind the signature. The competitor had done their capture work. My client had written a proposal.

A tender is not a writing exercise. It is the culmination of a relationship, a strategy and a team that knows what it wants to achieve — and how.

01

The hidden cost of lost tenders

Every lost tender has a cost that goes beyond the missed revenue. There are the direct hours of the bid team — an average of 200 to 400 hours per serious submission. The account manager's time. The cost of external expertise.

For an organisation that submits eight times a year with an average bid cost of €80,000, the annual investment is €640,000. At a win rate of thirty percent, you win €192,000 worth of bid effort — and write off €448,000. Those are the visible costs. The invisible ones are greater: the client you will not see again for four years, the competitive position your competitor now occupies, the organisational fatigue that sets in when teams structurally lose without understanding why.

The win rate is an outcome. The cause lies in the six months before the deadline — in every decision that was or was not made.

02

Capture: winning before the pen hits paper

Most bid teams start their work when the procurement notice appears. The best bid teams are already months into it by that point. Capture planning is the systematic work of building client intelligence, influencing specifications and positioning your organisation as the most logical choice — long before the formal process begins.

1
Build client intelligence
Who are the decision-makers? What are their personal success criteria — beyond the formal evaluation criteria? What pain do they want to solve? Without answers, you write a generic proposal for a client you do not know.
2
Influence the specifications
Tender documents are written by people who receive information from suppliers and advisors. Organisations that actively participate in market consultations influence the rules of the game in their favour. That is not gaming the system — it is professional practice.
3
Go/no-go as a strategic decision
The best bid organisations say no more often. They apply sharp qualification criteria: is the client known, is the opportunity realistic, is there a distinctive proposition? Selectivity raises the win rate and lowers the cost of bidding.
4
Know your competitive position
Who is likely to bid? What are their strengths? A win strategy without competitive analysis ignores half the playing field.
03

From process-compliant to strategically winning

Most bid teams deliver a compliant proposal. They answer all the questions, meet all the requirements — and present a submission the client cannot technically reject, but that does not convince them either. Compliance is the entry ticket. Not a differentiator.

Process-compliant
Compliance approach
  • Answers questions as posed
  • Describes what the organisation does
  • Generic themes, repeatable
  • Strong on technical completeness
  • Weak on client-specific relevance
Strategically winning
Win strategy approach
  • Drives the entire document on theme
  • Speaks the client's language
  • Client-specific win themes throughout
  • Strong on distinctive value
  • The client recognises their own situation

A proposal without a win strategy is a catalogue. The client flips through it, finds it tidy, and chooses someone else.

Win themes are short, specific and distinctive. They are grounded in what you know about the client — not what you want to say about yourself. They run from the executive summary through to the technical appendices.

04

The bid machine: one team, one goal

The most common cause of weak bids is not a writing problem — it is a collaboration problem. Sales brings in the tender and hands it over. Bid support writes the proposal. The client intelligence that could have made the difference stays locked in the account managers' heads. In the best bid organisations, that handover simply does not exist.

↗
Content planning & delivery
A strong proposal is not written — it is planned. Who writes which section? When does the strategic review happen? Bids finalised under time pressure are visibly weaker. Planning is quality assurance.
↗
Bid leadership as a strategic role
Every winning tender has an owner who is more than a project manager — someone who guards the win strategy, knows the client and keeps the competitive lens sharp. Bid leadership is a strategic role. Organisations that understaff it pay the price.
↗
Negotiation as an extension of the bid
The submission is the start of the conversation. Organisations that enter the negotiation phase without a strategy, fallback positions and a mandate structure lose at the last moment what they built in the proposal.
05

The learning process most organisations skip

After a lost tender, the most common reaction is: move on to the next one. The evaluation gets pushed back — and disappears. That is the most expensive decision a bid organisation can make.

1
Debrief after every loss — always
Always ask for a debrief. What worked, what fell short, why did the competitor win? Four questions are enough: What resonated? What was missing? Who made the decision? What could we have done differently? That conversation is the best market intelligence you can get.
2
Debrief after every win — too
Won tenders contain equally valuable information. Which win theme made the difference? Which section scored above expectation? Those insights are the building blocks of your next winning proposal.
3
Institutionalise the learning cycles
A bid library with strong sections, a sector knowledge document, a fixed retro format after every tender. Learning is not automatic — it is a choice that needs to be organised.
06

What AI changes in the bid process

AI in bid management

What changes: content production. AI generates standard sections, CV paragraphs and compliance responses in a fraction of the time. That is a genuine productivity gain — but only if the win strategy is already in place. Deploying AI to write the wrong thing faster means losing faster.

What also changes: client analysis. AI can summarise public information about a client — annual reports, press releases, procurement history, board changes — and identify patterns that capture teams miss. Direct gain in the positioning phase.

What does not change: the win strategy itself. AI cannot replace human insight into what a specific decision-maker truly needs, what their political context is within their own organisation, and why they should trust you. That insight is the product of relationships and presence — and it is precisely the insight that distinguishes the winning bid from a strong loser.

07

The choice to win structurally

A higher win rate is not a matter of working harder. It is a matter of positioning more intelligently, strategising more sharply and learning more consistently.

The organisations that win structurally do so because they start earlier, know the client better, run an explicit win strategy, operate sales and bid support as one team, and convert their experiences into structurally better performance.

"Tenders are not won in the final week before the deadline. They are won in the months before — by the organisation that is better positioned, better prepared and has learned better."

— Amir Bercovitz

Three questions for every bid organisation:
1. Do we know the client well enough to speak their language in our proposal?
2. Does every proposal have an explicit win strategy that drives the entire document?
3. Do we structurally learn from every tender — won and lost?
